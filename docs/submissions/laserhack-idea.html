1 · Problem statement

101 MIndians living with diabetes (ICMR-INDIAB, Lancet D&E 2023)
136 Mmore with prediabetes, most of them undiagnosed
BMI 23is where overweight starts for Asian Indians, not 25

India's diabetes and obesity epidemic is driven by everyday food: large portions of white rice, refined flour, fried snacks and sweet chai. Yet the tools people have do not fit them:

  • Food apps are built for Western diets. They don't know pesarattu, bisi bele bath or kadhi, and can't understand “do roti aur dahi” or “rendu dosai”.
  • Risk is mis-measured. South Asians develop diabetes at a lower body weight and with more belly fat, so global BMI cut-offs reassure people who are already at risk.
  • Generic advice fails. “Eat less rice” doesn't stick. Swaps for foods people already eat, in their own regional cuisine and within their budget, do.
  • Privacy and cost. Many users won't upload meal photos and health data to a server, or pay for a dietitian.

2 · Proposed solution

ThaliSense is a free web app that turns what people already eat into a personal, explainable plan. Everything, including the AI, runs inside the user's browser.

  • Log by typing, in your own words. A meal parser covering 108 Indian dishes understands quantities, Hindi and Tamil number words, regional names, plurals and typos.
  • Log by photo, even a whole thali. An on-device vision model (87.8% top-1, 95% top-3 on 1,694 test photos) scans the plate for every dish and suggests the usual sides. One tap confirms.
  • Know your real risk. The validated Indian Diabetes Risk Score (MDRF), BMI with Asian Indian cut-offs, and waist-to-height ratio, with clear advice to get tested when the risk is high.
  • An explainable coach agent. It reviews the past week, finds the biggest problems (carb-heavy plates, low protein, sugary drinks), and proposes swaps with the weekly calorie, protein and fibre impact of each.
  • A self-checking 7-day plan. Regional menus (South, North, East, West; veg, egg or non-veg) within a ₹/day budget. The agent verifies every day against the targets and repairs any misses, and every step is shown to the user.
  • Private, installable, offline. No account and no server: data stays on the phone, and it installs like an app.

4 · Real-world impact & feasibility

Impact

  • Earlier detection. IDRS flags high-risk people (score of 60 or more) and pushes them to a cheap fasting-glucose or HbA1c test.
  • Measurable change. On the demo week the coach finds swaps worth about 1,200 kcal a week (around 0.7 kg of fat a month) while raising protein and fibre.
  • Inclusive. Regional, vegetarian-aware and budget-aware (default ₹200 a day), in the words people actually use.
  • Scales to the public system. An ASHA-worker mode could screen whole villages offline on one phone.

Feasibility

  • Already working. Live with typed and photo logging, thali detection, risk scoring, progress tracking, the coach agent and 7-day plans.
  • Near-zero running cost. Static hosting; all compute happens on users' devices, so a million users cost the same as ten.
  • Measured accuracy. 87.8% top-1 and 95.0% top-3 on 1,694 photos from three datasets; whole-thali scanning doubles the dishes found.
  • Next (Round 2). Plate segmentation for crowded thalis, Hindi and Tamil voice logging, and an ASHA-worker screening mode.

ThaliSense is a wellness guide, not a medical device. It does not diagnose; it encourages users at high risk to get tested.
